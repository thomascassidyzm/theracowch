# What makes an elicitation deep — and what WAYL would have to ask

> Position paper, 2026-09-06. Written after Tom's ruling that the daily coaching line is the
> user's own words, quoted back, produced by their own "What are you like, anyway?" run. That
> ruling makes the asking the product: a thin answer at assessment is a thin week, every week.
> The brief was to say what makes a question get a person to say something they did not already
> have ready, using Tom's own method as the evidence, and then to give the actual questions.
> Nothing in the live assessment was changed. Quotes are Tom's unless marked; sources are the
> 2014 Reasonable Wealth WhatsApp transcript (RW1), the canon assembly in the tomcassidy-site
> repo, and the code in this repo.

---

## 1. The position, in one paragraph

A question is deep to the degree that its answer cannot be **retrieved** and has to be
**composed**. Everything a person has ready is a retrieval: their self-descriptions ("I give up
too easily"), their goals, their values, their CV. Those are rehearsed, and most of them are
script. Composition is forced when a question fixes two coordinates the person has never held
at the same time: a **concrete moment they recognise**, and a **seat they do not normally sit
in**. Tom's method is, almost entirely, a set of seat-changes. Two further conditions decide
whether composition happens or the person freezes: **permission** (the answer need not be true or
defensible — Helpful > True) and **cost** (the smallest version, one line, borrowed words allowed
— Choose easy). WAYL already supplies the moment and the permission; that is why it works as a
mirror. What it never does is change the seat. Every one of its thirty questions is answered from
the doer's chair. Add the chooser's chair at the right three points and the sentence composes
itself.

## 2. The evidence: how Tom's questions actually work

### 2a. The Gateway Question is never asked cold

Read the 2014 transcript and one thing is constant: "What WOULD you choose?" is always hung on
a sentence the person has *just said*.

> Yasmin: "Thing is I give up too easily"
> Tom: "Would you choose to give up easily?"
> Yasmin: "its ..easier..to give up sometimes"
> Tom: "Aha, but would you choose for it to be easier to give up sometimes? Is that you? Or is
> that your script?" — RW1, 28/02/2014 17:31–17:33

The question needs an anchor. The anchor is a self-description the person has already committed
to out loud. Then the subjunctive — WOULD, "if you were choosing" — splits the speaker in two:
the one who has the thought and the one who would choose it. The person has hundreds of stored
sentences about the first and none about the second. So the answer has to be made on the spot.
It is also cheap to make: Yasmin's reply to the first Gateway Question in the group is
"Nope :-) to both questions". Depth and effort are not the same axis.

The instrument's definition of the gap is Tom's, verbatim: *"the truth of you is how you would
CHOOSE to see yourself, not how you DO see yourself. Everything else is your script."*
(actors-agents-architects essay.) That is a measurable. WAYL currently measures the DO half only.

### 2b. The other seat-changes

Every distinctive move in the method relocates the speaker:

| Move | The seat it puts you in | 2014 wording |
|---|---|---|
| What WOULD you choose? | the chooser | "Would you CHOOSE to think that? (If you were choosing what to think?)" |
| If you were her mum | the witness | "If you were Yasmin's mum — anyone — would you choose that for Yasmin?" |
| The perfect day | the inhabitant of a specific unlived moment | "You wake up: what time is it? What can you see, where are you? What's the first thing you do?" |
| Not NOT done | the one looking back | "What do you want to have not NOT done, when you look back over your life?" |
| Is it helpful? | the user of a thought, not its prover | "Is it helpful, to think that you're not good enough right now? What might be more helpful?" |
| If money was sorted | the person with the constraint removed | "if money was sorted would you carry on doing your current job?" |

Watch the Jeni exchange (RW1, 28/02/2014 17:01–17:10). The opening question is broad: "what
would Reasonable Wealth look like for you?" She answers with a paragraph anyone could have
written: "Having made the right choices, invested in the things that are important to me…" A
retrieval. Tom does three things. He compresses it to one of her own words: "So, it's mostly
about time for you Jeni?" He removes a constraint: "if money was sorted would you carry on…?"
And she says the real thing: grand babies, travel, four and a half years to retirement. Then he
drops her into a moment: "You wake up: what time is it? What can you see?" The depth did not
come from a longer question. It came from a shorter one, asked from a different chair, about a
moment small enough to see.

### 2c. Helpful > True is the permission slip

Ask someone "what are you like?" and their script vets every candidate answer for truth: is
this really me, can I defend it, what if I'm wrong. That vetting is what sends people back to
the stored, safe sentences. Tom's discriminator lifts the gate:

> "Of course, it is possible that my way of looking at things isn't true. But let's not focus on
> whether it's true or not for a second. Ask yourself a better question: Is it helpful?"

And the live form, on the exact question of a self-belief the person could not prove: "Even if
you didn't think it was true… What would you choose? Helpful > True" (RW1, 05/03/2014 17:52).

This matters for the ruling more than anywhere else. WAYL is a truth instrument — "answer from
the gut", "what you'd actually do first" — and it should stay one; the mirror is honest because
it never asks you to aspire. But the sentence you will hear back for a year is not a truth
claim. It is a helpful sentence. If the product asks for it as a truth ("what will you do?") the
script will edit it down to something safe, and a safe sentence is a thin sentence.

### 2d. Choose easy is the fitness function on the answer

> "Ok, would you choose to be able to focus on one thing at a time for just a week, without it
> being a big effort?" — RW1, 03/03/2014 10:05

> "Choose easy! Not hard. Hard is hard. Who'd choose that????"

Two consequences. First, the ask itself must be tiny: one tap, one line, borrowed words allowed.
Second, the sentence must be tiny in what it asks of the person, because a sentence heard back
fifty times is either a companion or a reproach. The bank already contains the failure case:
*"February 9th. The resolution — real, chosen, yours — has been flat on the canvas for eleven
days now."* A resolution quoted back on 9 February is a nag. What survives is the version of the
sentence that is about choosing, not about performing — see §4.

### 2e. The literature agrees, for once

From `docs/so-what-layer.md` §2b: merely holding a goal does nothing (d ≈ 0.14); only completed
small behaviours move anything; and implementation intentions — "if I'm in situation X, then I
will do Y" — are the one framing with a large, replicated effect (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006,
d = 0.65). An if-then anchored to a recurring moment is exactly what a scenario plus a Gateway
Question answer produces. The method and the evidence point the same way.

## 3. What WAYL has, and what it lacks

**Has.** Thirty concrete, recurring, recognisable moments — the yoghurt, the 6:30 alarm, the bill
split, the silence after a risky message. Options written in a permission-giving voice with no
negative labels, several of them already in the chooser's register: *"The decision was made last
night — 6:30-you is just the courier."* *"Restart tonight. Day one is any day you say it is."*
*"…let the rest stay loose on purpose."* An honest exit with a free-text line. A one-way reveal
at 10, 20 and 30 that settles each ten so it cannot be tuned to the picture. This is a good
instrument and none of it should change.

**Lacks.** Any moment where the person is asked from the chooser's seat. The 08-29 trace
(published today) put it flatly: *"the asking never happens. The Gateway Question is never
actually put to the person by the assessment — they are given options and scored. A quotation
with no original utterance behind it is not a quotation."*

The only place in the estate where someone writes their own words for a wheel is the unlinked
`build-your-wheel` prototype, and it makes a different choice: it offers three *authored* stances
per spoke and asks the person to edit one. That is the right shape (never a blank canvas) built
on the wrong anchor. The stances are the app's guesses about the person. WAYL already holds
something better: the person's own tap, thirty times over.

**The structural point.** In 2014 Tom needed the person to voice a thought before he could ask
"would you choose that?". WAYL gets the person to voice thirty. Each tap is a Yasmin sentence —
"this is what I'd do" — and the Gateway Question is sitting next to every one of them, unasked.
The scenario is the anchor the Gateway Question has always needed. It is already built.

## 4. What WAYL would have to ask

Not thirty more questions. Three, each asked at a reveal, each in three beats. The taps stay
honest because the ten is already settled when the chooser's question arrives — the existing
one-way door is doing exactly the right job.

### Beat 1 — the person selects the moment (after each ten settles)

> **Ten moments, settled.** One of them comes round again and again. Which one would you most
> like to meet differently next time?

The ten scenarios, each with the option they tapped underneath. One tap. The person, not the
engine, decides where the script pinches. This is the selector move and it costs nothing.

An honest exit here too: *"None of them — these ten are fine as they are."* That is a real
answer and it is data: a ten with no pinch is a ten where DO and WOULD-CHOOSE already agree.

### Beat 2 — the Gateway Question, on their own tap

The chosen scenario again, their tap marked plainly:

> **What you'd do:** Renegotiate from inside the duvet — and evening-you loses the case about
> half the time.
>
> **If you were choosing** — not what you'd do, what you'd *choose* — which one?

The same four options, re-offered, plus *"This one, actually — I'd choose it again"* and
*"Something else."* One tap.

Three things fall out of this beat without asking anything extra:

- Where the WOULD tap differs from the DO tap, that gap is Tom's definition of the script,
  measured on the person's own words, in a moment they picked.
- Where the person taps *"I'd choose it again"*, that is the authentic self at work, and it is
  worth hearing back too — not as a task but as a fact about them they may never have said aloud.
- The chosen option's text is now the seed for Beat 3. It is already in the right register
  because the bank was written that way.

If the answer is "I don't know", Tom's own bypass is the follow-up, and it should be the only
one: *"What would happen if you did know?"*

### Beat 3 — the words

> **Say it the way you'd say it to a friend.** One line. This is the line you'll get back, in
> your words, exactly as you type it.

A textarea seeded with the option they chose in Beat 2. They can keep it, change a word, or
rewrite the lot. Then the confirm-back that the Reason-Ability engine already has: *"Is this
exactly what you'd want to hear?"* Yes, or edit. What is kept is verbatim, keyed to the
scenario id.

Blank is allowed. A blank keeps the option text and marks it `borrowed`, so the daily line can
be honest about it: "you picked these words" rather than "you said".

### What comes back, and how

The daily line is two parts. The **cue** is the scenario, in the bank's words — the stage
direction. The **line** is theirs — the only voice on it:

> 6:30. Mid-November. Dark.
> **"The decision was made last night. I'm just the courier."**

The cue is not the coach speaking. It is the *if* half of an implementation intention, and it
was the person's by selection. Without it the sentence loses its trigger and becomes an
aphorism. With it, it lands on the morning it is about. Flagged as a call for Tom in §6 all the
same, because it is the one place where non-user words touch the daily line.

### The acceptance test for a sentence worth a year

Five checks, applied to the line before it is blessed. Copy can say them out loud to the
person or just enforce them by shape.

1. **It names a moment that comes round.** Redemption latency short: an alarm, a bill, a text,
   a fridge. Never "be more disciplined".
2. **It is spoken as the chooser, not as a promise.** "I'd choose the courier" survives a
   snoozed alarm. "I will get up at 6:30" is falsified by one Tuesday.
3. **It survives a bad week.** The test to put to the person: *read it as if you'd just done the
   other thing.* If it stings, it is a resolution; shorten it or soften it until it doesn't.
4. **It can be said to a friend in one breath.** The 280-character cap in the Reason-Ability
   page is about right; shorter is better.
5. **It is theirs.** Verbatim, confirmed, never tidied. The engine's rule already: *"Never
   rewritten, tidied, capitalised or wrapped in encouragement."*

### Why three and not thirteen

Three sentences a person actually composed beat thirteen they nodded at. The wheel needs
thirteen spokes; it does not need thirteen sentences on day one. The honest fallback for a spoke
with no sentence is the authored bank, tagged `source: 'bank'` — and every bank fire is a
measure of an asking that hasn't happened yet. The same three beats can then run **once a
week** inside the app: one scenario from the remaining bank, the Gateway Question, one line.
That is the elicitation continuing at the wheel's own tick rate, and it is how a person reaches
thirteen of their own sentences by the end of a lap without ever facing a form.

## 5. Why the obvious moves make it worse

**A longer form.** Every added question is answered from the same chair, so it harvests more
retrievals. Thirty moments already give a resolution the results page can defend; forty would
give more fatigue and the same sentences.

**Open questions at the end.** "What do you want to work on?" is the broadest possible ask from
the doer's seat. It gets Jeni's first paragraph — true, generic, nobody's. The depth in that
exchange arrived only after Tom cut it to one of her words and moved her chair.

**Authored stances to edit.** Better than blank, and the wheel prototype was right to reach for
it. But an authored stance is the app guessing; the person's own tap is the person telling.
Seed from the tap.

**Asking for a goal.** Goals are the most rehearsed retrieval a person owns, and the literature
says holding one does nothing. The sentence is a choice in a moment, not a destination.

**Asking during the ten.** Would contaminate the taps: once a person knows a "what would you
choose" is coming, the DO answers drift aspirational. The reveal is the right place because the
ten is already locked.

## 6. Where I am unsure

1. **Whether people will type at all, on a phone, mid-run.** The ranked variant exists because
   even *choosing* was hard for some testers; typing is harder. The mitigation is that the
   whole design is two taps and an optional edit, seeded, three times. But the honest-exit
   textarea has been live for weeks and I found no telemetry on how often anyone uses it. That
   number would settle this and it is worth getting before building.
2. **Whether hearing your own sentence daily wears or nags.** Check 3 above is my best guard
   and it is a guess. Tom's own felt experience of receiving a blessed goal on the
   Reasonable Eating page is the only data point, and it is one person for one week.
3. **Whether the DO/WOULD gap reads as being caught out.** The whole method depends on "make it
   OK", and a results screen that says "here is where you don't do what you'd choose" can read
   as a verdict if the register slips. The bank's tone rule — attention maps, not verdicts, no
   negative labels — has to hold on this screen harder than anywhere.
4. **The cue line.** Whether the scenario stem counts as the coach's voice on the daily push, or
   as the person's by selection. My position is the latter; it is Tom's call.
5. **Single-choice or ranked.** I would build on the single-choice variant first: it already has
   the honest exit, the textarea plumbing and the settled-ten reveal. The ranked variant's
   "most like me at the top" is still a DO ranking, so the same three beats would work there
   later, but it has no free text today.
6. **Cross-device.** Everything WAYL keeps is device-local and the daily push is a cron on a
   server. The 08-29 trace names this and the Reason-Ability repo has the endpoint-hashed
   identity pattern that solves it. Not this document's problem, but the sentence is worth
   nothing if it cannot reach the morning it is about.

## 7. Summary for the redline

- Depth is the answer having to be composed, not retrieved. Composition needs a concrete moment
  plus a seat the person does not usually sit in; permission (Helpful > True) and low cost
  (Choose easy) make it possible rather than paralysing.
- WAYL has the moments and the permission. It has never once changed the seat.
- Three questions, each at a settled reveal, each in three beats: pick the moment, answer the
  Gateway Question on your own tap, say it your way. Seed the words from their tap, not from an
  authored stance.
- The sentence is a choice in a recurring moment, spoken as the chooser, short enough to say to
  a friend, and able to be read after a bad day without flinching. Verbatim, confirmed.
- Three real sentences on day one; one more a week after that, by the same three beats.
