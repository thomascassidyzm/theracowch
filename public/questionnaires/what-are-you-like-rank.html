What are you like, anyway?

The ranked one. Thirty little moments everyone knows, four ways to handle each — put them in order, most like you at the top.

The party where you only know the host. The yoghurt past its date. The bulb that’s gone and the ladder that’s buried. You already know what you’d do — you do it every day. This just pays attention.

Nothing here is right or wrong. You’re not picking a winner and you’re not committing to anything — just putting four ordinary reactions in order, going on what you’ve actually done before. Most likely at the top, least likely at the bottom. If two feel close, put them next to each other and move on; that’s exactly what a middling ranking is for.

Nudge a row up or down with the arrows until the order feels right. Most likely at the top, least likely at the bottom.

While you answer, nothing moves. No bars creeping, no score ticking — what you can’t watch, you can’t steer, and that’s what keeps the answers honest.

Then, at 10, 20 and 30 moments, the curtain lifts: six things about you come into focus — the familiar five, plus risk appetite, the one everybody argues about later. Sharper at every look.

Four of them are relative: put 100 people in a room — you’d be standing around here. Risk appetite and sensitivity are ours rather than borrowed, so there’s no crowd to stand in yet — those two come back as a reading on their own scale instead. No types, no verdicts, no right answers.

Nothing is locked in as you go: step back and forward through the ten you’re on, re-order anything, pick up one you skipped. Once the curtain lifts on a ten, though, that ten is settled — you can’t go back and tune it to the picture.

This isn’t a test and it isn’t a diagnosis — it’s a mirror, and a playful one. Everything you order stays on your device. Bring anything that catches you to your chat with Mandy.

What you’re like — at today’s resolution

So — what are you like?

How to read this: for the familiar four — openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness — put 100 people in a room. Those bands show where you’d most likely be standing, and how sure we can honestly be. A wide band means we’d need more answers before narrowing it; that’s the truth, not a flaw.
Risk appetite and sensitivity work differently. Those two are ours, not borrowed from an established instrument — so there is no room of 100 behind them. We’d be inventing the crowd. What you get instead is an absolute reading: where your own answers put you on each axis’s own scale, with the same honest width. Where you sit on it, not where it puts you among other people.
Read together: your familiar-five position, how you handle risk, and how loudly your alarm system runs — taken together that’s a starting point, not a verdict. Where it tends to earn its keep is anywhere two people have to fit together: a relationship, or a business with someone. Not a prediction of how either will go. Just a better set of questions to ask early — and earlier than you’d otherwise think to ask them.
A note on honesty: personality measures — all of them, including this one — explain only a modest slice of what a person actually does. Treat everything above as an indication worth exploring, never a label. Where a band sits somewhere that surprises you, that gap between how you see yourself and the order you actually put things in is the single most interesting thing on this page.
